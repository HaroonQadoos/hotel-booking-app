STEP 01

The entity model — what owns what

Four documents. A Hotel owns many RoomTypes; a room type owns many Bookings; a User holds many bookings. The load-bearing field is RoomType.totalUnits — the allotment. You are not selling room 237, you are selling one of the twelve Deluxe Doubles, and which physical room the guest gets is decided at check-in or never.

Booking denormalises hotel even though it could be reached through roomType, so "every booking at this hotel" is one indexed query instead of a join. And pricing is a snapshot, not a reference: it copies the rate, currency and night count at the moment of booking, so raising the room rate next week cannot silently re-price a reservation someone already holds.

STEP 02

Half-open dates — why back-to-back stays don't collide

A hotel stay is a run of nights, not a span of time. Jun 1 → Jun 3 is two nights — the night of Jun 1 and the night of Jun 2 — and the guest is gone by the morning of Jun 3. So the interval is half-open: [checkIn, checkOut). The checkout date is a boundary, not an occupied night.

That is what makes the overlap test strict on both sides. Two stays collide only if each one starts before the other ends, and a stay checking out on Jun 3 does not block a stay checking in on Jun 3 — they merely touch.

Worth guarding with a test. A ≤ on either side of that predicate is the single most likely bug in this module. It never fails on random dates — only on stays that touch — so it survives casual testing and then rejects real guests at the seam between two reservations.

STEP 03

Why subtracting the overlaps is wrong

The obvious way to answer "is there a room free?" is to take the allotment and subtract every booking that overlaps the requested dates. It is wrong, and it fails in the direction that loses you money: it rejects stays that are perfectly bookable.

Here is the smallest case that breaks it. A room type with two units, one booking on the night of Jun 1, another on the night of Jun 3, and a guest asking for Jun 1 → Jun 4. Both bookings overlap the request, so the sum is 2, and 2 − 2 = 0. But look at the nights: not one of them is full.

The fix is to stop treating occupancy as something you add up across a range. Occupancy is a per-night quantity, and what constrains a stay is its worst night:

available = totalUnits − MAX over each night in [checkIn, checkOut) of occupied(night)

STEP 04

The sweep line — computing peak occupancy

Taking the maximum over every night sounds like it needs one row per night. It doesn't. Occupancy only changes at a check-in or a check-out, so you sweep the boundaries: emit a +units event at each arrival and a −units at each departure, sort, and walk them keeping a running total. The highest value that total reaches is the peak. Five steps, and the cost is the number of overlapping bookings — not the length of the stay.

The tie-break is not cosmetic. A and B are a legal back-to-back pair — the room is occupied every night, but never twice. Process the arrival before the departure and the running total spikes to 2 for an instant that does not exist in reality, and the sweep reports negative availability for a room type that is merely full.

STEP 05

POST /bookings — the full request pipeline

Everything above becomes one request. The guard chain runs first and cheapest, then the stay rules, then the part that has to be atomic. Note the third guard: EmailVerifiedGuard is new, and it closes a gap that exists in the codebase today — isEmailVerified is currently written at sign-up and then never consulted anywhere. A booking is the right place to start demanding it, because a confirmation sent to an unverified address goes nowhere, and a reservation the guest cannot prove they hold is worse than a refusal.

The guard reads the user record back rather than trusting the token: AuthUser carries no verification claim, and a token signed before the user clicked the link would be stale for up to a day.

STEP 06

The overbooking race, and why a transaction alone loses it

Checking availability and then inserting is check-then-act, and two requests can interleave between the two halves. The instinct is to wrap it in a transaction — and that instinct is wrong here, which is the whole reason this step exists.

MongoDB gives snapshot isolation, and a write conflict is raised when two transactions modify the same document. Two bookings are two different documents. There is no shared row for them to fight over, MongoDB has no range locks to notice that they both depend on a count they each read, and overlap cannot be expressed as a unique index. Both transactions commit, perfectly happily, and the room is sold twice.

Bookings of different room types never touch the same counter, so they stay fully parallel. Only concurrent attempts on the same room type serialise — which is exactly the set that needed serialising.

This needs a replica set. Atlas is one, so deployment is fine. A standalone local mongod throws on startSession().withTransaction(), and the failure surfaces at booking time rather than at boot, which makes it confusing. Either run a single-node replica set locally or point dev at Atlas — and say so in the README.

STEP 07

Cancellation — inventory returns by itself

Cancelling flips a status and stamps an audit trail. It does not delete the row, and it does not decrement anything. Both of those follow from the same design choice: bookings are the single source of truth for occupancy, and the sweep in step 04 only counts bookings whose status is pending or confirmed. A cancelled booking stops matching that filter, so its nights simply stop producing events. There is no counter to keep in sync and therefore no counter that can drift.

The ownership test is the same rule UsersController already applies — admin, or your own record. That is why the design pulls assertCanActOn out into src/auth/ownership.ts: a duplicated authorisation check is how two copies quietly stop agreeing.
